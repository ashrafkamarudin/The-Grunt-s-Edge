The Grunt's Edge
World Setting: Imperial Military and Feudal Structure
Status: Accepted working canon
Scope: The empire's political hierarchy, military organization,
noble forces, recruitment obligations, and standard military service.
1. The Imperial State
The empire is a centralized imperial state with a powerful aristocracy
and a medieval noble hierarchy.

The emperor is the supreme political and military authority. However,
the great noble houses retain substantial land, wealth, local influence,
and administrative power.

The empire is not simply a loose collection of independent noble
territories. It has central institutions, laws, administration, and a
unified military command. At the same time, noble influence is deeply
embedded in governance and military leadership.
The Emperor
- Holds supreme authority over the empire and its armed forces.
- Appoints or approves senior imperial commanders and officials.
- Can issue imperial military orders and impose obligations on the
  nobility.
- Must still account for the political power and interests of major
  noble houses.
The emperor is powerful, but cannot necessarily disregard the
aristocracy without political consequences.
The Nobility
Nobles govern territories, collect revenues, administer local affairs,
and maintain their own households and estates.

Their obligations to the empire may include: - Taxes and financial
contributions. - Food, equipment, transport, horses, and other
supplies. - Recruitment quotas or other forms of military support. -
Local infrastructure and defensive preparations.

Noble houses vary in wealth, influence, military tradition, and loyalty.
Their interests may align with the emperor's, conflict with one
another's, or diverge from imperial policy.
2. The Unified Imperial Army
The empire has one official Imperial Army, serving the emperor and
operating under a common military command.

A soldier serving in this force is an imperial soldier, regardless of
which territory or noble house originally supplied the recruit.

The Imperial Army has: - A unified chain of command. - Standardized
ranks and military regulations. - Common training and organizational
practices. - Central military planning and deployment authority. -
Official service records and military law.

Its responsibilities include: - Defending imperial borders. -
Maintaining strategic fortresses and garrisons. - Protecting important
roads and trade routes. - Conducting authorized military campaigns. -
Responding to monster incursions. - Organizing seasonal reinforcement
and frontier deployments.

The army is a central imperial institution, not a collection of
unrelated private armies.

The empire maintains a permanent professional force. It can expand
through recruitment obligations, volunteers, and other authorized means
when additional manpower is required.
3. Noble Household Forces
Nobles may maintain smaller forces associated with their households,
estates, and personal security.

These forces are distinct from the unified Imperial Army. They are not
automatically independent field armies.

A noble household force may include: - Household guards. - Knights and
mounted retainers. - Estate guards. - Small professional infantry
units. - Personal escorts. - Military attendants and staff.

Their ordinary responsibilities include protecting the noble's
household, property, estate, and local interests.

They may be called upon to support imperial operations, but large-scale
campaigns require imperial authorization. Household forces may defend
their own estates and respond to local threats within the limits of
imperial law.

The empire places restrictions on the size, equipment, and independent
military activity of noble forces. These restrictions help prevent
powerful noble houses from developing into rival states with
unrestricted armies.

The exact restrictions may vary by rank, privilege, historical treaty,
or imperial decree.
4. Nobles in the Imperial Army
Nobles frequently hold senior positions in the Imperial Army. This is a
normal feature of the empire's military and political structure.

A noble might serve as: - A general commanding a large formation or army
group. - A regional commander responsible for a military district. - A
fortress or garrison commander. - A cavalry officer. - An officer
commanding an infantry regiment or other unit.

Noble birth does not automatically guarantee competence. Some noble
officers are capable and experienced; others benefit from family
influence, political connections, wealth, or imperial favour.

Commoners can also rise through the ranks. Their advancement is
possible, but may be more difficult because of class prejudice, limited
access to education, patronage networks, and aristocratic influence.

This creates a continuing tension between military merit and
aristocratic privilege.

The Imperial Army is unified in command and organization, but its
leadership and internal politics are shaped by the noble class.
5. Imperial Military Obligations
Military obligations flow from the emperor through the imperial
administration and the noble hierarchy.

A typical chain of responsibility is:
1. The Emperor: Establishes imperial policy and holds supreme
   military authority.
2. Imperial Military Command: Determines military requirements,
   recruitment needs, deployments, and strategic priorities.
3. Regional Nobles and Governors: Receive obligations for their
   territories, including quotas, taxes, supplies, and military
   support.
4. Local Lords, Landowners, and Settlements: Fulfil the obligations
   assigned to them through recruitment, resources, or approved
   alternatives.
A village such as Eastwood does not ordinarily receive a personal order
from the emperor. Its obligation comes through the regional authority
responsible for its territory.

The regional noble receives an imperial quota and distributes the
obligation among settlements under their jurisdiction. The village elder
or local authority then has to fulfil the assigned requirement.
Recruitment obligations
The empire maintains a professional standing army, but it can also rely
on regional recruitment obligations to expand its forces.

Depending on circumstances and local rules, a settlement may be able
to: - Provide a suitable volunteer. - Select a recruit through a local
agreement. - Fulfil its obligation through an approved financial or
material alternative, where permitted.

The exact rules can vary according to imperial policy, regional
conditions, and the severity of the military situation.

During a military emergency, the imperial command may increase
recruitment quotas, accelerate deadlines, restrict alternatives, or
require a greater share of available manpower.
6. Standard Imperial Military Service
Commoner recruits may enter the Imperial Army through a fixed-term
service contract.

The standard arrangement relevant to the story is a two-year Imperial
Service Contract.

The two-year term begins on the date of formal enlistment, not the date
the recruit leaves their village.

The contract is a standard arrangement for commoner recruits. Noble-born
officers may enter through a different commission or service
arrangement.

The possibility of extension should not be treated casually. It requires
a specific legal or imperial justification.
7. Military Training
The Imperial Army's ordinary training process is longer than two weeks.
New soldiers are expected to learn discipline, formations, weapons,
field procedures, and how to function within a unit.

The exact duration and intensity may vary according to branch, role,
experience, and military circumstances.

A compressed fourteen-day training period is an emergency measure, not
the normal standard for all recruits. It may be used when the army needs
replacements quickly, but it should be understood as producing minimally
prepared soldiers rather than fully trained veterans.
8. Setting Guardrails
- The empire has one unified Imperial Army, not a collection of
  independent noble armies.
- Noble household forces exist, but are smaller and subject to
  imperial restrictions.
- Nobles commonly occupy senior military positions, while commoners
  can also rise through the ranks.
- Noble birth does not guarantee competence.
- Military command and organization are centralized, while funding,
  recruitment, and leadership remain influenced by noble obligations
  and privilege.
- The emperor is powerful, but the great noble houses retain
  meaningful political leverage.
- Commoner recruits may serve under a two-year Imperial Service
  Contract.
- A fourteen-day training period is an emergency measure, not ordinary
  full soldier training.
Core model: A centralized imperial state with a feudal aristocracy.
One Imperial Army, noble influence throughout its command structure, and
regional obligations connecting imperial authority to local settlements.
