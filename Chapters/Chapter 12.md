# Chapter 12 — The Healer's Tent

I wake up before I fully want to, ribs still there as that dull pressure that makes breathing feel slightly negotiated every time I do it. I stay still for a moment, letting it settle before I sit up, because I’ve learned by now that rushing it just makes everything worse for a few seconds longer than necessary.

When I finally move, pain pulls through my chest halfway and I pause again, holding that position until it eases enough for me to finish the motion properly. Ren is already awake when I manage it, looking over at me without much expression like he already knows what I’m going to say.

“How’s it?”

“Still hurts.”

He just nods once, like that confirms the expected state of the world.

“Yeah. Take it easy today. No morning training anyway.”

He shifts slightly where he sits, relaxed in a way that makes his own injuries feel like something background rather than urgent, like they’ve already been filed away as something to deal with later. Darrin already went out, he adds after a moment, and that pulls my attention up a little more than it should.

Of course he did.

I don’t answer, and Ren doesn’t expect one. He just leans back again, staring at the tent ceiling like the day has already run out of things worth reacting to. Outside, someone coughs — long, wet, not clean — and I lie back for a moment longer without moving, not resting, just not participating.

Magic… I wonder how I can learn it.

I don’t sleep properly after that, just drift in and out until the noise outside shifts slightly, becoming less chaotic and more structured, like the camp has already decided it will function whether anyone is ready or not. When I sit up again my ribs still complain, but it’s manageable now, not better, just something I can work around without thinking about it too much, and I push the flap open and step outside.

The air hits differently out here, warmer than inside the tent but heavier at the same time, like the ground never really dries anymore and just keeps holding onto yesterday. I start walking without a clear direction, letting my feet decide for me, and somehow end up at the training area without really remembering choosing it.

At first it looks empty, just flattened dirt and the faint marks of repeated movement, until I see Darrin already there with a sword in hand, no armor, just repeating the same motion over and over again — step, cut, reset — the same angle each time like he’s trying to carve it into his body instead of his memory. I stop and watch for a while without calling out, and he doesn’t react, or maybe he just doesn’t care enough to break rhythm, which feels more like him anyway. After some time I realize I’ve been standing there too long and move on.

The camp is properly awake now, people moving between tents but without urgency, more like maintenance than preparation, like everything that mattered already happened and what’s left is just keeping things from falling apart too quickly. That’s when I start noticing things I didn’t really register before — soldiers walking worse than I am, bandages already dark with old blood, one man sitting against a crate just staring at nothing while someone adjusts his leg without asking him anything at all. I slow down without meaning to, looking at them longer than feels comfortable, and for a moment I catch myself thinking I’m not that bad compared to them, but even as I think it it doesn’t feel true in the same way anymore.

Magic comes back again, uninvited, and I find myself walking toward the medical tent before I’ve really decided to do it.

It’s louder before I even reach it, not shouting, just constant movement, fabric shifting, low voices overlapping without ever fully settling into silence. I stop near the entrance where knights are standing, not inside but close enough that it feels controlled, like they’re deciding who gets in and who doesn’t. One of them looks at me like I don’t belong here, which is probably correct, but I look past him anyway.

Inside, I see it.

Light — faint, unstable, not like anything physical being held or cast. It flickers around someone’s hands pressed over a wounded arm.

The wound reacts to it.

Not instantly, but in a way that makes my stomach tighten — torn flesh drawing itself back together, edges closing as if something inside the body has suddenly decided it should not be open anymore. Blood slows, then stops, and the skin knits over it in uneven, rapid movement that doesn’t feel natural even if it clearly is. The soldier on the table tenses once, jaw locking, then slowly goes still. Not relief. Not quite pain either. Just… absence of whatever was wrong before.

I forget to breathe properly for a second.

Then the knight at the entrance snaps his attention onto me.

“Move, soldier. If you’re not injured or helping, get out.”

I step back immediately.

“Ah— sorry.”

My voice comes out too fast, and I almost turn away, almost let it end there, but something inside doesn’t settle with it. Inside, someone groans again, another voice gives a short command, and the light flickers once more.

Not useful. I know that.

But I still point slightly toward the entrance instead of leaving fully.

“I can help. Just… small stuff.”

The knight doesn’t look convinced, but after a moment he gestures sharply anyway.

“Don’t get in the way.”

That’s it.

Permission, I guess.

I step inside more carefully this time, keeping my ribs steady, trying not to look like I don’t belong here, because I don’t, but I stay anyway, and for now I just watch.

I step inside and immediately stop.

For a moment I just stand there, like my body forgot what it was supposed to do next. Too much movement, too many voices, too many bodies packed into one space that feels like it’s only holding together because no one has the time to let it fall apart.

A few steps in, someone is already looking at me.

He's wearing a robe. Not white. Not really. It used to be something close to it, maybe, but now it’s closer to a pale grey that’s been worked over too many times. Thick linen, rough at the seams, sleeves pushed up without care. Stains layered into it in places that don’t wash out anymore — dried brown, darker patches near the front, fresh smears still damp in others.

He moves fast, like the person wearing it doesn’t have the luxury of standing still for long.

“Hey. You free?”

A hand gestures toward a stretcher without slowing down.

“If you’re not hurt, give me a hand with this one. Bandage him.”

I hesitate for half a beat too long, then step forward anyway.

The man on the stretcher looks worse up close. Not dying in a way that’s obvious, but in that quiet middle ground where everything depends on what happens next and nobody is confident about the outcome.

I swallow once.

“I can bandage.”

My voice sounds unsure, but it doesn’t matter. The robe just pushes cloth into my hands.

“Then do it. You’ll figure it out.”

No further instruction.

I kneel carefully, keeping my ribs steady as I work. Every time I lean forward, a dull ache reminds me not to overreach. Pressure first. Wrap tight enough that it holds, not so tight that it cuts circulation. I remember that much. The rest comes slower, but it comes.

By the time I finish securing the bandage, the healer is already moving again.

“Good. You’re not useless.”

That’s the only acknowledgment I get before he turns away.

I don’t even have time to answer before he’s already at the next cot. I hesitate, then follow.

Not because I’m told to. Just because standing still suddenly feels wrong.

The healer doesn’t slow down for me. He just talks as he works.

“Hold that leg. Don’t let it drop.”

I move in before I fully process it, gripping the injured man’s shin while the healer unwraps a blood-soaked bandage.

“Like this?” I ask, unsure.

“Higher,” he says immediately. “If you let it hang, swelling gets worse.”

I adjust.

The man on the cot groans.

“Stop moving it,” the healer snaps, not at me, but at the patient. Then, to me, quieter but still sharp, “Don’t brace with your wrist. Use your forearm. You’ll lose grip if he jerks.”

I switch.

My ribs complain, but I keep position. I shift my weight to find a better angle. It helps. A little.

Another patient is pulled in beside us. The healer glances once.

“Cloth behind you. Sack on the left.”

I hesitate.

“I don’t know where—”

“Behind you. Sack. Left side.”

I turn fast, grab it, pass it over.

No praise. No pause.

Just work continuing.

My ribs pulse each time I bend. It makes me hurry. I should know better. At one point, I wrap too loosely — the cloth slips slightly as I tie it off.

The healer notices without even looking fully.

“A bit tighter. It’ll slip otherwise.”

I redo it.

A short silence follows, only the sound of breathing and fabric shifting.

Then, as he presses his hands over another wound and a faint flicker of light pulses between his fingers, I speak without thinking.

“That… is that magic?”

“Focus,” he says immediately. “Hold pressure there.”

I shut my mouth and press down where he points.

But a moment later, as the patient steadies, he answers without looking at me.

“Don’t stare at it. It drains you more than it helps.”

I nod slightly, even though he probably isn’t watching.

Time stops feeling clean after that.

There is no clear beginning or end to tasks. Just bodies coming in, instructions thrown at me, mistakes corrected without ceremony. I learn quickly that “good enough” is whatever stops the bleeding long enough for the next problem to be handled. I try to move faster, but my ribs won’t let me. Every crouch is a negotiation.

At one point, I ask quietly while passing cloth,

“Do you always work like this?”

The healer snorts once.

He didn't answer.

By the time the light outside shifts, I’ve lost track of how many times I’ve knelt, stood, pressed, wrapped, or been told to move something slightly higher or lower. My hands smell faintly of iron and damp cloth that never fully dries.

When there’s a rare pause, the healer finally looks at me properly.

“You’re not one of the attendants.”

It’s not a question.

“Not really,” I say.

He grunts.

“…your ribs.”

I stop.

“Sit.”

No room to argue. I lower myself carefully, and the moment I do the pain in my side tightens like it’s been waiting to be noticed.

He presses once.

I flinch.

"Cracked," he mutters.

His hand lifts and light flickers between his fingers, faint at first, then steady enough to settle over my ribs. It doesn’t feel like healing. Not really. More like the pain being pressed back into place, held together instead of allowed to fall apart. The ache loosens, and breathing stops catching.

A few seconds pass.

Then it fades.

He pulls his hand back.

I inhale deeper, testing it.

Better.

But something hits right after.

A heaviness. Sudden. Like the strength gets pulled out from under me and I only notice it when I try to move.

My shoulders feel late. My arms heavier than they should be.

I stay seated a moment longer than necessary.

“…I thought you don’t heal levies,” I say.

It comes out slower than I expect.

He looks at me.

“Yeah. Normally, we don’t.”

A beat.

“You were useful today. Consider it a reward.”

His eyes flick once to my side.

“Eat properly tonight. Don’t skip meals.”

I blink slightly.

“Good work today.”

A tap lands on my shoulder as he turns away.

I sit there for a moment longer, then stand — slower than I want to admit — and start walking back.

The camp feels quieter now. Not peaceful. Just drained. My ribs don’t catch on every step anymore. It takes a few breaths to notice that properly.

---

I eat dinner without tasting it. Some kind of stew, bread that’s harder than it should be. I finish it because my body needs it, not because I want it. Afterward, I find my spear and walk to an empty patch near the tents. I test a few thrusts — slow, careful, waiting for the familiar pull in my ribs.

It doesn’t come.

I push harder. A full extension. Then another. My ribs hold. No pain. No hesitation.

I stand there for a moment, breathing evenly, the spear still in my hands.

*'Healed.'*
