# Chapter 11 — The Morning After

I wake up on the second day, and for a moment I don’t immediately remember where I am, only that everything feels heavier than it should, like my body is arriving a little later than my awareness.

The ceiling above me is canvas stretched tight over wooden poles, dirty and slightly damp in places, and I just lie there staring at it while my ribs slowly remind me they are still intact, my arm follows after that, wrapped and heavy in a way that makes it feel like it doesn’t fully belong to me anymore, and then my head starts to pulse in small, dull waves that get worse whenever I try to focus.

Somewhere nearby, someone coughs hard enough that it turns into a wet, strained wheeze that doesn’t fully recover.

I stay still for a while because moving doesn’t feel like something my body is ready to negotiate yet.

Outside the tent, there is already movement, boots on packed earth, metal clinking, low voices that rise and fall without structure or calm, and nobody sounds surprised by any of it, like this level of exhaustion and noise is just what the world is supposed to be now.

This is normal here.

Later, the healers arrive.

I hear them before I see them, not because they announce themselves, but because people shift out of their way without hesitation, not out of respect exactly but more like habit, like everyone understands there are things you do not interrupt unless you want trouble.

They move from cot to cot quickly, checking wounds, pressing fingers into bandages, lifting cloth just long enough to see what’s underneath, speaking in short exchanges that I can’t always make out clearly over the noise around them.

When they reach me, one of them presses two fingers into my ribs through the bandage, pauses for a moment as if listening for something I can’t feel, then checks my arm and finally looks at my face like I am just another case on a list.

“You’re stable.”

That’s all they say at first, and for a moment I wait for more, because it feels like there should be more.

After a second, the same healer adds without looking at me again, “No more treatment needed. Move.”

I blink.

“…move?”

But they are already gone, already stepping to the next cot, already forgetting I was ever spoken to.

I sit up slowly after that, my body protesting in small, sharp reminders along my ribs and shoulder, but nothing stops me anymore, it just makes everything feel heavier than it should be, like gravity has decided I am not fully worth the effort.

No one stops me when I leave the tent.

Outside is worse than I expected.

During my walk back to the tent, I notice the camp stretches further than I thought it would, rows of tents scattered across uneven ground with no clear structure anymore, and people are everywhere in a way that makes it hard to tell where one group ends and another begins, some sitting, some lying down, some just leaning against whatever they can find because standing properly seems like a decision they are no longer willing to make.

A few are still wearing armor, not because they haven’t taken it off yet, but because they don’t look like they have the strength to.

Dirt, blood, sweat — it all just sits on them in layers that don’t look temporary anymore.

I stop walking without meaning to.

“What…” I say before I fully realize I’m speaking out loud, my voice sounding smaller than I expect. “What happened here?”

A voice answers from somewhere nearby without even turning toward me.

“You woke up late.”

I turn.

Ren is lying back against a pack that looks like it’s barely holding its shape anymore, his armor still on and stained, his hair damp with sweat and dust clinging to it like it gave up trying to fall off.

He looks at me for a second, then gestures slightly at the camp around us like it should explain itself.

“You think this gets cleaned up in a day? Battle ended, yeah. Doesn’t mean everything resets.”

I look around again, slower this time, trying to match what he is saying with what I’m seeing, but it still doesn’t fully connect.

“We just… stay like this?”

Ren gives a short, tired snort.

“We stay alive like this.”

I swallow, my mouth dry in a way I didn’t notice until now.

“How long was I out?”

He shrugs slightly, like time itself is not worth measuring too precisely anymore.

“Not long. Day and a half maybe. You went down first evening. Got smashed by one of those hounds.”

The word hits a little late in my head.

Smashed.

I try to remember it properly, but it only comes back in fragments, weight, impact, shield slipping, then nothing after that.

“Darrin pulled you out,” Ren adds, like it’s just another detail in a long list. “If he wasn’t close, you’d still be part of the line.”

My throat tightens slightly before I can stop it.

Darrin.

I turn my head, scanning without thinking, until I find him further down the camp sitting alone, cleaning a blade with slow, methodical movements, like the rest of the world doesn’t require his attention right now.

“…I don’t know how to thank him.”

“Quit it.”

The voice comes from Darrin himself without him looking up.

“It’s what anyone would’ve done.”

No emphasis. No weight. Just something stated as fact and left there.

I don’t answer because there isn’t anything clean to say back.

Ren shifts slightly where he is lying, wincing as he adjusts his weight.

“You’re lucky,” he says after a moment, quieter now. “Most people don’t wake up after going down like that. Especially not levies.”

“Levies?”

He looks at me like I should already know, but there’s no annoyance in it, just tired acceptance.

“Us. The ones not worth the expensive stuff.”

That lands differently than it should.

I don’t respond immediately.

Ren exhales slowly, staring up at the sky like he is trying not to think too hard about anything.

“If they’d used proper healers… half of us wouldn’t look like this.”

Something shifts in his tone, not anger exactly, more like something worn down over time until it doesn’t have sharp edges anymore.

“Proper healers?”

He tilts his head slightly.

“Yeah. Mages. Clerics. Whatever you want to call them.”

That word sits wrong in my head for a second.

Mages.

It doesn’t fit anything I’ve seen so far. Shields, mud, blood, exhaustion, bodies that barely keep moving.

“…they exist?”

Ren lets out a short laugh, almost without humor.

“You really got knocked out bad, huh.”

He adjusts his position slightly, then continues.

“They can close wounds in minutes. Broken bones too. Flesh, whatever. If they bother.”

I look down at my bandaged arm without really meaning to.

It is still there.

Still hurting.

That shouldn’t make sense.

“…why don’t they just heal everyone?”

Ren’s expression flattens a little, like he’s said this kind of thing before too many times to care about dressing it up.

“Because they don’t.”

Simple. Final. No explanation needed beyond that.

A horn sounds somewhere deeper in the camp, not an alarm, just structure returning in pieces, movement resuming like the world is forcing itself back into order even if everything inside it is still broken.

I stand there for a while longer than I should, trying to fit the word mage into everything I’ve seen so far, but it doesn’t settle properly anywhere.

Not yet.

And I don’t know if that’s because I don’t understand it, or because it doesn’t belong in the same world I thought I was in.

---

Night settles over the camp in a way that doesn’t feel peaceful, only quieter than before. Most of the tent had gone quiet by then, not in the sense of true silence but in the way a battlefield camp only ever softens into something less violent rather than truly calm, with distant voices still passing outside the canvas, boots dragging through dirt, and the occasional sharp command from somewhere far beyond my sight.

Only Darrin and I were left inside.

I was still lying back carefully because of my ribs, the healer having dismissed me earlier but not really fixed anything, just decided I was no longer worth attention, and every breath I took reminded me of that difference in a way I couldn’t ignore.

Darrin looked better than me, already cleaned up, only minor cuts left, sitting with that loose exhaustion people get when their body has stopped panicking but hasn’t quite forgiven them yet.

For a while neither of us spoke.

Then I broke it.

“Hey, Darrin… can you tell me about magic?”

He turned his head slowly, like he wasn’t sure he had heard me correctly.

“Magic?”

“Yeah.”

He just stared for a second longer than comfortable before frowning slightly.

“What, your village never talked about it?”

I let out a small breath, thinking about it, then shrugged.

“Not much.”

That wasn’t entirely true, not really, but explaining Earth stories, movies, games, all of that felt pointless in a way I couldn’t even begin to translate into words here, so I left it as it was.

Darrin shook his head like he couldn’t quite believe it.

“That’s hard to imagine.”

He shifted his weight against the crate behind him and stretched his legs out a little, settling into a more comfortable position as if the question had reminded him of something distant.

“Back home, that was all we talked about when we were kids. Heroes, knights, grand mages, dragon slayers… every traveler who passed through had at least one story like that, some tale about a mage wiping out an army or a hero standing alone against something impossible.”

A faint smile pulled at his mouth, not mocking, just remembering.

“I wanted to be one of them.”

I glanced at him.

“A hero?”

“Sure.”

He gave a small, almost amused exhale.

“What kid doesn’t, right?”

I couldn’t argue with that, so I just hummed quietly in agreement.

“When you’re young, you think you’ll be the one doing impossible things,” he continued, gaze drifting up toward the tent ceiling as if he could still see those old stories there, “then you grow up and realize most people don’t become heroes.”

He paused, then added a little more quietly, “Most people just stand behind them instead.”

There wasn’t bitterness in it, just something more grounded, like he had tested the idea against reality enough times to stop arguing with it.

I shifted slightly, careful of my ribs.

“So magic is real then.”

He gave me a look, like the question itself was strange.

“Yeah, of course it is.”

“I mean… I saw the healing, but it still feels— I don’t know. Different.”

He snorted lightly.

“You’re strange.”

I didn’t answer right away. Something in me stirred for the first time since I woke up in that forest. If magic was real — if I could learn it — then maybe this wasn’t just about surviving. Maybe this was actually going to be fun.

“...Thanks.”

“Welcome.”

We let it sit for a moment again, the tent creaking softly around us.

Then I asked what was actually on my mind.

“Is there a way to learn it?”

That made him pause longer, thinking rather than answering immediately.

“I don’t know about ‘learn’ like you’re thinking.”

“You don’t know?”

“I’ve never met a mage properly,” he admitted after a moment, scratching lightly at his jaw as if trying to pull the answer from memory instead of certainty, “just seen a few from far away, once or twice… that’s about it.”

I frowned.

“Then how does it work?”

He exhaled through his nose.

“Well… from what I’ve heard, every few years some envoy comes through villages and tests people, usually the ones around sixteen or older.”

“Tests them for what?”

“Affinity,” he said, then hesitated slightly, “or talent. Something like that.”

“You never got tested?”

“No.”

He shook his head once.

“I was too young when they came through, and they never came back after that.”

That last part came out almost casually, but there was something underneath it that I couldn’t quite place, something that felt like a door that had closed a long time ago and never reopened.

“What happens if someone has it?” I asked.

“They take them,” he said simply, then added after a beat, “I guess.”

“You guess?”

He gave me a look.

“Eric, I’m from a village, not a mage tower.”

That made me laugh a little despite everything.

“I’m serious,” he continued, gesturing faintly with one hand, “nobody explains this stuff to people like us. Everything I know comes from old men, drunk merchants, and travelers trying to sell stories along with their ale.”

I leaned back again, letting that settle.

“So… we could still have it?”

Darrin thought about that for a moment, then shrugged.

“Maybe.”

Then, after a pause, he added, “But nobody from my village ever got picked.”

The words weren’t heavy, just factual.

Still, they stayed in the air longer than expected.

After a while I spoke again, quieter this time.

“Maybe we should ask someone.”

“Ask what?”

“If they can test us.”

He turned his head toward me slowly.

Then he laughed, not in a mean way, just in a way that made it clear how unrealistic it sounded.

“Us?”

“Why not?”

“We’re levies.”

I frowned slightly.

“So?”

“So nobody’s going to waste time checking two exhausted levy soldiers because they suddenly got curious about magic,” he said, like it was the simplest truth in the world.

I opened my mouth to argue, then stopped.

Because he wasn’t wrong.

Darrin leaned back, letting out a long breath.

“If you’ve got magic in you, it picked a really bad time to show itself.”

I let that sit for a moment, staring at the tent ceiling again, the idea of magic no longer feeling distant or mythical, just… somewhere above us, existing in a world that clearly didn’t care whether we understood it or not.

Eventually exhaustion caught up again, heavier than curiosity, and the tent slowly faded into something softer around the edges as I stopped thinking about it too hard.
