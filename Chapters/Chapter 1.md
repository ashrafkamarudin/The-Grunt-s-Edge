# THE GRUNT'S EDGE
## Volume I: The Price of Staying

# Chapter 1: The Snap

The convenience store door chimed as Eric stepped outside, its cheerful electronic announcement following him into the evening before the automatic doors slid shut. The familiar hum of refrigerators and canned pop music disappeared behind the glass, leaving him with the quieter sounds of the street.

He adjusted the plastic bag hanging from his fingers and glanced inside. One tuna mayonnaise onigiri and a bottle of green tea. Dinner, or at least something that would pass for it.

He hadn't felt particularly motivated to cook lately. Not that he had ever been much of a cook to begin with.

The evening was pleasantly cool. A faint breeze moved between the buildings, carrying the smell of damp pavement and something fried from a restaurant farther down the street. The convenience store's fluorescent lights spilled onto the pavement behind him, illuminating cracks in the concrete and a few cigarette butts gathered near the drain. Across the road, a vending machine hummed beside an apartment entrance.

A car passed, its headlights sweeping briefly across his face before disappearing around the intersection.

Eric checked his phone.

9:47 p.m.

Nothing.

He slipped it back into his pocket and started walking.

His apartment was about fifteen minutes away, following a route he could probably navigate with his eyes closed. Left at the intersection, straight past the shuttered ramen shop, then through the small park running alongside the river. He had walked it so many times that the journey rarely required any thought.

He opened the onigiri as he walked, peeling away the wrapper and taking a bite. The rice was slightly firm around the edges, and the seaweed had lost most of its crispness from sitting under the convenience store's heat lamps. It wasn't particularly good, but it was edible.

He chewed and continued down the pavement.

A cat watched him from the roof of a parked car, its eyes briefly reflecting the streetlights. Eric glanced at it, but the animal showed no interest in acknowledging him. It had probably seen enough people walking home with convenience store dinners to know better than to expect anything.

The ramen shop appeared at the next corner, its shutters pulled down for the night. Eric turned toward the park.

The iron gate stood open, as it usually did. Beyond it, a paved path curved between patches of grass and several old trees. The streetlamps cast a warm orange glow across the walkway, although the light grew weaker toward the riverbank, where bushes and benches disappeared into the shadows.

A jogger passed him, breathing steadily as his shoes slapped against the pavement. Eric moved slightly aside to let him through, then continued toward the water.

It was quiet for a Friday night. Most people were probably at home, having dinner or watching television. Others were at bars or restaurants, spending their evenings somewhere more interesting.

Eric preferred the park.

There was a small slope near the riverbank where the grass was reasonably well maintained. It was far enough from the path that people rarely disturbed him, and the view of the water was pleasant enough when he didn't feel like looking at his phone.

He sat down, placed the plastic bag beside him and finished his onigiri.

The river moved steadily against the embankment. Across the water, apartment windows formed broken reflections that stretched and trembled whenever the current disturbed the surface. Somewhere in the distance, a car door slammed, followed by a brief exchange of voices.

Eric twisted open his bottle of green tea and took a drink.

Nothing particularly interesting had happened today. Work had been work. His commute had been uneventful, and the convenience store clerk had given him the same polite greeting she always did.

It was an ordinary Friday.

He should have been thinking about what to do over the weekend, whether he needed to buy groceries, or if there was anything worth watching when he got home.

Instead, his attention remained fixed on the river.

There was a familiar heaviness in his chest tonight, a feeling he had never quite managed to explain. It wasn't sadness exactly, nor was it loneliness, although it often arrived alongside both. It was more like a quiet awareness that something was missing.

Fourteen years.

That was how long he had been here.

Fourteen years since the night his life had changed, and yet there were still evenings when the memory returned with an intensity that surprised him.

Eric leaned back on his hands and looked toward the sky. The glow from the city drowned out most of the stars. A few brighter points managed to pierce through the haze, but he couldn't identify any of them with confidence.

He had never been particularly interested in astronomy.

The village where he was born had been different. There had been no streetlights, no electric signs or buildings tall enough to block the horizon. At night, the sky had been dark enough for the stars to fill it, and the only light near the houses came from torches, braziers and the occasional lantern.

He remembered the smell of smoke drifting between thatched roofs. Livestock shifting in their pens. The sound of his mother humming while she worked.

The memories were old, but they had never completely disappeared.

He had been six when everything ended.

He could still remember the village well, the uneven stonework around its rim and the wooden bucket that always seemed too heavy for him to lift. He remembered playing nearby, although he could no longer recall exactly what he had been doing.

Then came the screams.

They had been close. Much closer than anything a child should have to hear.

He remembered running, though he couldn't remember whether he had been running toward someone or away from something. His father's hand had closed around his arm, gripping tightly enough to hurt. There had been shouting, the sound of breaking wood and something heavy moving through the village.

He remembered seeing the monster only briefly.

A mass of black chitin and too many legs, moving between the houses with terrifying speed. It had been larger than any animal he had ever seen, and the sight of it had frozen his thoughts before his body remembered how to run.

Then there had been the light.

Not white, not yellow. It was a colour he had never been able to describe properly, something that seemed to exist between the colours his eyes understood. It had spread through the village without a sound, accompanied by a pressure that made the air itself feel heavy.

His father's hand had slipped from his.

The monster, the houses, the shouting and everything around him had disappeared.

The next thing Eric remembered was pavement.

He had awakened on a sidewalk in a city he didn't recognize, naked and shivering, with traffic rushing past and strangers shouting in a language he couldn't understand. He had cried until a woman stopped to help him. She wrapped him in her coat, took him to a police station and stayed with him until someone could figure out what to do.

After that came the shelter, the orphanage and years of trying to understand the world he had somehow landed in.

He learned the language. He learned to read. He went to school, eventually found work and grew old enough to live independently. Life had moved forward whether he understood what had happened or not.

He had spent a considerable amount of time searching for answers.

History books, mythology collections, academic papers and even the more questionable corners of the internet had all received their share of his attention. He had looked for stories about missing people, strange lights and unexplained disappearances. Anything that might suggest he wasn't the only person to have experienced something similar.

He had found plenty of strange stories.

Nothing that explained his own.

Eventually, the searching became less frequent. There were bills to pay, work to attend and ordinary responsibilities that demanded more attention than an impossible event from childhood.

He had made a life here.

A modest one, perhaps, but it was his.

Eric looked down at the bottle in his hand. Condensation had gathered around its surface, leaving his fingers slightly damp.

*I shouldn't be thinking about this.*

He had asked himself the same questions thousands of times. Why had it happened? What had caused the light? Had his family survived? Had the village even remained standing?

There had never been an answer.

Sometimes he wondered whether his parents had searched for him. Whether his older brother had remembered him. Whether his little sister had grown up hearing stories about the brother who disappeared before she was old enough to know him.

He knew almost nothing about what had happened after he vanished.

That was perhaps the hardest part.

His memories of them had survived, but the people themselves existed only in a past he couldn't reach.

Eric took another drink and set the bottle beside him.

The grass was slightly damp beneath his palms. The evening breeze carried the smell of river water and asphalt, and the distant hum of traffic continued beyond the park.

He leaned farther back, eventually lowering himself onto the grass.

He was tired.

Work had been particularly tedious that week, and he hadn't slept especially well the previous night. His apartment was only fifteen minutes away, but the thought of getting up, walking home and deciding what to do with the rest of his evening seemed unnecessarily troublesome.

He could rest for a moment.

Just a minute.

The grass pressed softly against his back as he settled into a more comfortable position. Above him, the sky was a dull mixture of grey and orange, with only a handful of stars visible through the city glow.

He closed his eyes.

The river continued moving nearby, its steady murmur blending with the distant traffic.

For a while, there was nothing else.

Then sleep took him.

***

The first thing Eric noticed was the cold.

It was not the mild evening chill he remembered from the park. This cold was sharper, moving freely across his exposed skin and drawing heat from his body. A faint breeze passed over his back and legs, raising goosebumps along his arms.

He shifted, trying to pull the grass closer around himself.

Something hard pressed against his shoulder blade.

His brow furrowed.

The ground beneath him felt uneven. Small stones and what seemed like dry leaves were scattered across the surface, while something narrow and solid pressed against his lower back.

He opened his eyes.

For several seconds, he stared upward without understanding what he was seeing.

The sky was black.

Not the grey-orange darkness of a city evening, but a deep, uninterrupted black filled with stars. Hundreds, perhaps thousands of them, stretched across the heavens. They were bright enough to illuminate the outlines of the branches above him.

Eric blinked.

He turned his head slightly.

Trees.

Tall trees surrounded him, their trunks thick and irregular. Their branches reached overhead, shifting gently in the breeze. Beyond them, the darkness continued uninterrupted.

A faint rushing sound came from somewhere nearby.

Water.

He slowly pushed himself upright.

The movement sent a brief wave of dizziness through his head, but he managed to sit. His hands sank into damp earth covered with fallen leaves and small stones.

He looked down.

Then he looked again.

He was naked.

His hoodie, trousers and shoes were gone. So was the convenience store bag. His phone, wallet and everything else he had been carrying had disappeared.

Eric stared at himself for a moment, waiting for his mind to offer an explanation.

It didn't.

He turned in a slow circle.

The trees were too close together to be part of any city park he knew. Their trunks were thick, their roots spread unevenly across the ground, and the undergrowth was dense enough to make moving through it difficult. The air smelled of wet soil, decaying leaves and clean, fast-moving water.

He could hear the river more clearly now.

It sounded considerably larger than the one beside the park.

His breathing became shallow.

"Where..."

His voice sounded strange in the quiet.

He swallowed and tried again.

"Where am I?"

There was no answer.

He looked toward the river and then back at the trees, trying to establish some sense of direction. Perhaps he had wandered away from the park while sleepwalking. Perhaps someone had taken his clothes as a prank and left him in the woods.

The explanations were absurd, but they were explanations.

He needed something to hold on to.

Then he noticed the sky again.

The stars.

He had seen photographs of night skies taken in remote places, far from the light pollution of cities. This was brighter than those images, or at least it seemed that way to him. There was no familiar glow on the horizon, no distant outline of buildings, no aircraft crossing overhead.

He looked for the moon.

He couldn't see it.

A cold feeling settled in his stomach.

"No."

He looked down at his hands. Dirt had gathered beneath his fingernails, and a small cut crossed one of his fingers. He pressed his thumb against it, feeling the sting.

The sensation was immediate and unpleasant.

This wasn't a dream.

Or, if it was, it was considerably more convincing than any he had experienced before.

He took a slow breath and tried to think.

*I was in the park.*

He remembered sitting beside the river. He remembered finishing his dinner and drinking green tea. He remembered lying on the grass.

Then nothing.

*I must have been taken somewhere.*

But by whom? And why would anyone strip him naked and abandon him in a forest?

He looked around again.

There were no footprints that he could identify, no discarded clothing and no sign of a nearby road. Even if someone had carried him here, the location made no sense.

His mind began searching for possibilities, moving rapidly from one explanation to another.

*Am I still asleep? Some kind of hallucination? Did I have a medical episode?*

He pinched the skin on his thigh.

The pain made him wince.

"Great."

His voice sounded too loud. He glanced toward the trees, half expecting something to move in response.

Nothing did.

He rubbed his face and tried to steady his breathing. Panic wouldn't help. Whatever had happened, he needed to understand his surroundings before worrying about the rest.

First, he needed shelter.

Then water and clothing.

He glanced toward the river again. It was probably the best reference point available, although approaching an unfamiliar body of water in the dark wasn't exactly ideal.

He shifted his weight and prepared to stand.

That was when he noticed the feeling in his chest.

It had been there since he woke, though he hadn't paid attention to it. A strange sensation, difficult to distinguish from the anxiety spreading through his body.

He pressed a hand against his sternum.

Something felt different.

He looked around again, this time more slowly.

The forest was unfamiliar, but there was something about it that unsettled him in a way that went beyond simply being lost. The air, the darkness, even the sound of the river seemed to carry a familiarity he couldn't explain.

He knew that smell.

Wet earth. Leaves. Smoke from somewhere far away, faint enough that he might have imagined it.

His thoughts stopped.

He looked toward the river.

Beyond the trees, water moved over dark stones, catching the starlight in broken reflections. The sound was steady and natural, without the distant hum of pumps, traffic or machinery.

He had heard something like it before.

A long time ago.

A memory surfaced, not as a clear picture but as a collection of impressions. A small boy lying on grass. A wooden fence. The sound of insects in the evening. His mother's voice calling from the house.

And a sky filled with stars.

Eric's throat tightened.

He stood, unsteadily, and looked up.

The trees.

The soil.

The stars.

He had spent fourteen years trying to find a way back to a place he barely remembered. Fourteen years of convincing himself that the world he had come from was probably gone, that the people he remembered were probably dead, that whatever had happened to him was something he would never understand.

Yet now, standing naked in the middle of a forest, he felt something he hadn't felt since he was six years old.

The distance was gone.

That strange, persistent sensation of being somewhere he didn't quite belong had disappeared.

He didn't know how else to describe it.

The world around him felt familiar in a way his mind couldn't justify. Not because he recognized the trees or knew where the river led, but because something deep within him seemed to recognize the place itself.

His breathing slowed.

"No way..."

He looked down at his hands again.

They were larger than the hands he remembered from childhood. Of course they were. He was twenty now. Fourteen years had passed.

Fourteen years.

His stomach tightened as another thought forced its way through.

If this was truly his original world, then the village might still exist.

His home.

His family.

His father and mother.

His older brother.

Elysia.

His little sister would be fourteen now.

He remembered her as a baby, small enough to fit comfortably in his mother's arms. He remembered touching her tiny hand and being surprised when her fingers wrapped around his.

Would she remember him?

Would anyone?

He tried to imagine what he would say if he found them. How could he explain fourteen years in another world? How could he convince them that the six-year-old boy who had disappeared was now a grown man?

And what if they weren't there?

What if the village had been destroyed?

What if the monster had killed everyone?

His thoughts began spiralling again, and he forced himself to stop.

There was no point imagining answers before he knew where he was.

He looked toward the river.

Whatever else had happened, he was still alive. He had a body, two working legs and enough awareness to recognize that standing around naked in a forest was a terrible long-term survival strategy.

He needed to move.

Eric took a cautious step, then another. The ground was uneven beneath his bare feet, and the cold was beginning to make his toes ache.

He paused and glanced back at the patch of earth where he had awakened.

There was nothing there.

No sign of the park. No convenience store bag. No phone. Not even a trace of the grass he remembered lying on.

Only trees and damp soil.

He looked upward once more.

The stars seemed impossibly bright.

A quiet laugh escaped him, though there was little humour in it.

"Fourteen years," he muttered. "And this is how I get back? Haha ... shit."

He rubbed his arms and turned toward the sound of the river.

He didn't know where he was. He didn't know how far he was from the nearest settlement, or whether the forest was safe. He didn't even know whether the people he remembered were still alive.

But one thing was becoming increasingly difficult to deny.

He was no longer on Earth.

He was home.

And for the first time in fourteen years, Eric had no idea what to do next.
